Forecasting Methods

A poll asks what you think. A market asks what you're willing to bet. They're not the same question.

Polls and prediction markets both try to anticipate election outcomes. They measure different things. When they diverge, that's worth paying attention to.

Published December 15, 2026

What each method actually measures

A poll asks respondents what they think will happen, or how they plan to vote. It measures stated intentions and preferences at a moment in time. The respondent has no financial stake in being right.

A prediction market asks participants to put money behind their belief. The price reflects the aggregate financial commitment of people who each face real gain or loss based on accuracy. The incentive to be right is explicit.

That difference in incentive structure doesn't make one method universally better. It makes them different tools, each with specific failure modes. For background on how prediction markets work, see are prediction markets accurate.

What polls get right

Polls directly measure voter preferences. A well-designed poll with appropriate sampling, weighting, and question design can accurately reflect what a population intends to do. When polls systematically miss, it's usually a methodological failure (bad sampling frame, nonresponse bias, herding by pollsters) rather than an inherent weakness of the measurement approach.

Polls also capture information that markets can't directly access: how respondents feel about candidates, their certainty about voting, their demographic context. That granular data is useful for modeling even when the topline number misses.

Illustrative. Polls measure stated preferences; prediction markets aggregate financially-backed beliefs.

What markets get right

Markets can aggregate information beyond what polls capture. A trader with strong priors about electoral mechanics, turnout modeling, or late-breaking information can express that view through price. If a large body of informed traders consistently disagree with polls, that divergence is a signal.

Markets also update continuously. A poll from 3 weeks before an election reflects opinion at that moment. A market price from the morning of election day reflects the most current information set available to its participants.

Illustrative. When polls and markets diverge over time, the direction of that divergence is a signal worth examining.

When they disagree

The interesting cases are when polls and markets give different signals. In recent US elections, prediction markets have often priced candidates at higher probabilities than poll averages implied. Some of that reflects genuine additional information. Some may reflect participant biases in market populations (wealthier, more politically engaged, potentially skewed in other ways).

Neither source wins every time. Using both together, and paying attention to what specifically is driving the divergence, is more useful than committing to one method.

Illustrative. A combined signal using polls and market prices typically carries more information than either alone.

The practical read

When a prediction market and a poll average agree, that's a relatively robust consensus. When they diverge by more than a few points, ask why. Is the market reflecting genuinely different information, or is it pricing in biases and herding among a non-representative participant pool? Is the poll aggregating a large, recent, well-sampled dataset, or a few outlier surveys?

Frequently asked questions

Are prediction markets more accurate than polls?
In some settings, yes. In others, no. Neither wins universally. Markets tend to have an edge when incorporating late-breaking information or non-public signals. Polls have an edge when measuring voter intent directly in well-sampled surveys.
Why do prediction markets often show higher odds than polls suggest?
Multiple reasons. Market participants may genuinely have better information. Market populations may also be systematically skewed in ways that bias their probability assessments. Both effects can operate simultaneously.
What does it mean when polls and markets diverge significantly?
It means the two measurement approaches are reflecting different information sets. The right response is to investigate the divergence, not to automatically trust one over the other.
Can I use both polls and markets to forecast better?
Yes. Combining information sources reduces reliance on any single method's failure modes. FiveThirtyEight's model, for instance, historically incorporated prediction market signals alongside polling averages.
