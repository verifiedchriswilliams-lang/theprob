How-To Guide

73%. Here's what that number actually means.

Prediction market odds look like percentages. They're not quite that simple. Here's how to read them, what moves them, and when to trust them.

Published November 25, 2026

What a prediction market price actually says

A prediction market lets you buy and sell contracts tied to future events. The most common version is binary: a YES contract pays $1 if the event happens, $0 if it doesn't. The market price is the price at which buyers and sellers agree to trade.

A YES contract trading at $0.73 implies roughly 73% odds. Not because someone calculated it. Because that's what the market is currently willing to transact at.

That distinction matters. The price is not an authoritative probability handed down from above. It's the equilibrium of buyers and sellers under whatever conditions exist right now. For everything else you need to know about how these markets work, start with the Prediction Markets 101 guide.

Buy YES at $0.73. If the event resolves YES, you receive $1. Profit: 27 cents per contract before fees. If it resolves NO, you lose the 73 cents. That's the whole trade.

YES and NO

YES means the defined event occurs. NO means it doesn't. In a simple binary market, YES and NO prices add up to roughly $1. Not exactly, because fees, spreads, and order-book mechanics get in the way. But roughly.

The exact definitions live in the contract rules, not the headline. "Will Company X acquire Company Y before December 31?" and "Will Company X announce an acquisition of Company Y before December 31?" sound similar. They are not the same contract. Read the fine print before you read the probability.

Reading price movements

A market moves from 42% to 57%. That's a 15-percentage-point increase. Not a 15% increase. The crowd changed its pricing. The number does not, by itself, tell you why.

New data, a public announcement, a large trader repositioning, or a thin order book absorbing a moderate buy can all produce that move. The percentage shift is the headline. The story is what happened around it.

A sudden 20-point move on a high-volume market usually means someone knows something. The same move on a thin market might mean someone bought 50 contracts.

Volume and liquidity: the context behind the number

72% in a deep, active market tells you something. 72% in a market with 3 trades and a 10-point bid-ask spread tells you almost nothing.

Volume is the total amount traded over a period. Liquidity is how easily you can buy or sell right now without moving the price. They are related but not the same. A market can have high historical volume and no depth today.

The bid-ask spread is the gap between what buyers will pay and what sellers will accept. A wide spread means higher trading costs. It also means the displayed midpoint might not be executable at any meaningful size. For a full breakdown on this, see the guide on prediction market liquidity.

Quick check before trusting a market price: Is the spread narrow? Is the market actively trading today? Did this move come with meaningful volume behind it? Are other comparable markets moving in the same direction?

What 90% actually means

90% means the market is pricing the event as highly likely. It does not mean certain. It means: if you ran this exact scenario 100 times with these exact conditions, you'd expect it to resolve YES roughly 90 times.

That's calibration. And you can't evaluate it from a single outcome. A 90% market that loses was not necessarily wrong. A 60% market that wins was not necessarily lucky. For a deeper look at whether these prices hold up over time, read the article on prediction market accuracy.

One data point is not evidence. A 90% isn't a guarantee. It's a price. Treat it accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

What does a 70% prediction market probability mean?
The YES contract is trading around $0.70. The market is pricing the event at roughly 70% odds, subject to fees and trading conditions. It's not a guarantee. It's what buyers and sellers currently agree on.
Are prediction market odds the same as betting odds?
Related but different. A sportsbook publishes odds that build in a margin. Prediction markets express prices as tradable contracts. The implied probabilities can look similar, but the mechanics behind them are not the same.
Can prediction market probabilities change?
Yes, continuously. Prices update as participants trade in response to new information, changing expectations, and market conditions. A major news event can shift a price by 20 points in minutes.
Does higher volume mean more accurate odds?
Not automatically. Volume improves price discovery and makes the market harder to push around with a small trade. But it doesn't guarantee the crowd is right. High-volume markets have been wrong before.
Do I have to trade to use prediction market data?
No. The prices are public information. Reading them doesn't require placing a position. The Prob follows these numbers so you don't have to monitor 50 exchanges yourself.
